## 1. The Governing Idea

Elysian corrections rest on one sentence, which appears in the Criminal Floor
and in every regional code:

> **The deprivation of liberty is the punishment. Nothing else is.**

A person in custody retains every Charter right except freedom of movement.
They vote. They receive healthcare and education on the same terms as anyone
else. They correspond, receive visits, hold property, marry, and sue. Any
additional deprivation — of contact, of dignity, of access to daylight — is not
part of the sentence and requires separate legal justification.

There is a second idea underneath it, and it is demographic rather than moral.
With a median lifespan of 112 Elysian years (`hist.demographics` §1), a person
sentenced at 34 EY to the maximum 30 years is released at 64 EY with roughly
**fifty years of life ahead of them**. On Elysium, almost everyone comes back.
Corrections is therefore not primarily an ethical stance but an actuarial one:
the Concord will live with these people for half a century, and designs
accordingly.

## 2. Restorative Justice

Restoration is the default disposal for bands 1 and 2, and available at the
victim's option in bands 3 and 4.

**The conference.** A trained facilitator convenes the person responsible, the
person harmed, supporters of both, and where relevant a community
representative. The process establishes what happened, what harm resulted, and
what would go some way to repairing it. The outcome is a **restoration plan** —
apology, compensation, work, treatment, undertakings — which is registered with
the District Court and enforceable as a court order.

Three constraints define the Elysian version:

- **Victim consent is absolute.** No conference occurs without it, and a victim
  may withdraw at any point without giving reasons and without prejudice to the
  prosecution.
- **Admission is required.** Restoration is not available to a person who
  denies the conduct; they are tried. Restoration is not a route around the
  presumption of innocence.
- **No sentencing discount for participation is guaranteed.** A court may take
  a completed plan into account, but a person who declines restoration may not
  be sentenced more heavily for declining. This mirrors the Mediation House rule
  (`law.courts` §1): making refusal costly makes consent meaningless.

**Does it work?** Canon gives the honest figures rather than the advocacy ones.

| Indicator | Restorative disposal | Conventional disposal |
|---|---|---|
| Reoffending within 5 EY (bands 1–2) | 11% | 19% |
| Reoffending within 5 EY (bands 3–4) | 24% | 29% |
| Victim satisfaction with process | 81% | 44% |
| Compensation actually paid | 88% | 51% |

The reoffending advantage is real, modest, and smaller at higher bands. The
victim-satisfaction advantage is large and consistent, and Concord policy
literature is explicit that this — not reoffending — is restoration's strongest
justification. Roughly 12% of conferences fail to reach a plan and return to
ordinary sentencing.

## 3. Custody

| Indicator | Value |
|---|---|
| Detained population | 2.97 million |
| Rate | 41 per 100,000 |
| Held on remand | 9.4% (279,000) |
| Serving band 5 sentences | 6.1% |
| Median sentence served | 2.8 EY |
| Facilities | 38,400 |
| Maximum residents per facility | 120 |

**Small and local.** No Elysian facility holds more than 120 people, and
placement is within 200 km of the person's home Commune wherever possible —
achieved in 84% of cases. The design follows from the family structure: with
multi-generational households the norm (`hist.demographics` §4), a distant
placement removes a person from three or four generations at once.

**Life inside.** Residents work, at market wages, taxed normally, with earnings
divided between restoration payments, savings held for release, and their own
use. They study; 62% are enrolled in education at any time. They keep their own
clothes, cook, and manage their units collectively. Family contact is a right
and not a privilege: it may be restricted only by judicial order on specific
grounds.

**Separation.** Solitary confinement as a punishment is prohibited outright.
Separation for immediate safety is permitted for up to 24 hours on the
authority of the facility director, extendable only by judicial order, to an
absolute maximum of 72 hours, with medical review every 12 hours and mandatory
reporting of every instance.

Canon records that this is one of the least well-observed rules in Elysian
corrections. The Audit Service found 2,140 separations exceeding the limit in
the last reporting year, concentrated in nine facilities, and the finding
recurs. It is the clearest current gap between what the Concord's law requires
and what its institutions deliver.

## 4. Release and Reentry

**Graduated release** is the norm: a person moves through decreasing supervision
over the final third of a sentence, typically ending in open conditions with
daily work in the community. Sentences of any length are reviewed by a court
every 5 Elysian years, with the burden on the state to justify continued
detention (`law.substantive` §2.2).

**Reentry** is treated as an infrastructure problem. On release a person has:
guaranteed housing (Charter right 8, Phase 10), continuing healthcare including
any treatment begun inside, their accumulated savings, and a caseworker for
2 years. Employers may not ask about convictions unrelated to the work, and
convictions are spent — legally erased from ordinary disclosure — after periods
ranging from 2 EY for band 1 to 20 EY for band 5.

**Reoffending within 5 EY of release: 18%.** The Concord publishes this beside
its own target of 12%, which it has not met in forty years.

## 5. The Hardest Problem

Elysian law permits no preventive detention of the sane and no sentence beyond
30 years (`law.substantive` §2.2). Some people therefore reach the end of a
sentence while still, on any honest assessment, dangerous.

The Concord's answer is a **judicial supervision order**: a court may impose
conditions after release — residence, reporting, exclusion zones, treatment
engagement — but the order is time-limited (maximum 5 EY, renewable only on
fresh evidence of current risk), is itself appealable, may not amount to
detention, and must be the least restrictive measure adequate to the risk.
Roughly 9,400 people are under supervision orders at any time.

This does not solve the problem, and canon does not claim it does. A small
number of people are released each year whom the state believes likely to cause
serious harm, and some of them do. Between EY 380 and EY 412 there were 214
homicides committed by persons within 5 years of release from band 5 sentences.

The Concord's stated position, restated by the Constitutional Court in
EY 297, is that a power to detain people for what they might do is a power that
cannot be safely held by any state, and that the harm it would prevent is
smaller than the harm it would eventually cause. That judgment is not popular.
It has survived two referendum campaigns. Canon records it as a **live moral
disagreement in which the Concord has chosen a side and pays for it.**

## 6. Known Weaknesses

| Weakness | Nature |
|---|---|
| **Separation limits are breached** | 2,140 over-limit separations in the last year, concentrated in nine facilities; a recurring Audit Service finding and the clearest law-to-practice gap in corrections |
| **Reoffending target unmet** | 18% against a 12% target, missed for forty years without an agreed explanation |
| **Released dangerous persons** | 214 homicides in 32 years by persons recently released from band 5 sentences; an accepted and unpopular cost of refusing preventive detention |
| **Restoration under-serves serious harm** | The reoffending advantage narrows sharply at bands 3–4, exactly where the stakes are highest |
| **Placement failure for 16%** | Local placement is achieved in 84% of cases; the remainder are disproportionately from small Regions with few facilities |
| **Spent-conviction leakage** | Convictions are legally spent but informally remembered; the Record Office cannot erase what a community knows, and long lifespans make this worse than it would otherwise be |
