## 1. What the Charter Is

The Concord Charter is the constitution of the Elysian Concord, ratified by
simultaneous planetary referendum and in force from `EY-0001-M01-D01`. It is
short by the standards of its ambition — 41,000 words in each of the 41
registered languages, all equally authoritative (`cult.languages` §4).

The Charter's opening sentence is the most quoted text on Elysium:

> *We were not conquered into this. We agreed, having seen the alternative.*

That sentence is doing constitutional work, not rhetoric. No power won the Long
Emergency (`hist.timeline` §8), so the Charter could not be an instrument of a
victor, and every institution below is designed on the assumption that no
participant is strong enough to dominate and none needs to be.

## 2. The Four Structural Principles

**2.1 Decentralization by default (Article 3).**
All powers rest with the Regions except those the Charter explicitly assigns to
the Concord. The planetary tier has **enumerated** powers; the residual sits
below. Where competence is genuinely disputed, the Constitutional Court decides,
and its standing rule of construction is that ambiguity resolves *downward*.

The Concord's enumerated powers are deliberately few:

| Domain | Rationale |
|---|---|
| Atmosphere, ocean, and climate system | Physically indivisible |
| Planetary commons (deep ocean, polar ice, orbital space) | No region can own them |
| Defence, disarmament verification, and disaster response | Long Emergency lesson |
| Epidemic response and biosecurity | Pathogens ignore borders |
| Interregional transport, grid, and communications backbone | Network effects |
| Currency and planetary monetary policy | Phase 6 |
| Constrained List materials and strategic reserves | `planet.resources` §9 |
| Fundamental rights and their enforcement | Cannot vary by region |
| Space infrastructure and off-world territories | `space.infrastructure` |
| External relations and first contact | `dipl.external` |

Everything else — education, healthcare delivery, housing, policing, most
taxation, land use, culture — is regional or local. The Concord may set
**floors** (minimum standards regions must meet) but not **ceilings**, and may
not direct how a region meets a floor.

**2.2 The audit principle (Article 9).**
Every organ of government is audited by a body it cannot appoint, fund, or
discipline. This is why the independent offices of §6 exist and why their
funding is fixed in the Charter rather than voted annually. The principle
predates the Corran Scandal in text and was given teeth by it in EY 187.

**2.3 Ecological limits are constitutional (Article 12).**
Certain environmental thresholds are written into the Charter itself and cannot
be suspended by ordinary legislation, emergency decree, or executive act:

- Atmospheric CO₂ must remain within **320–360 ppm** (`planet.climate` §4).
- The abyssal plain, the Veydran Ice Cap, the Verdanne core, and the
  hydrothermal fields are inviolable (`planet.biosphere` §6).
- Fossil carbon may not be extracted for combustion; retained carbon is a
  constitutional trust (`planet.resources` §7).
- A sustained 20% weakening of the ocean overturning circulation automatically
  triggers a planetary environmental emergency (`planet.climate` §3).

The reasoning is stated in the Article: *a limit that can be suspended in a bad
year will be suspended in a bad year, and a bad year is when it matters.*

**2.4 Candour in government (Article 15).**
Deliberations are published, dissents are recorded, and every organ must publish
its own failures. The Public Record Act (`hist.timeline` §13) implements this.
Three specific requirements recur throughout the constitution:

- **Mandatory minority reports.** Any decision by any Concord body must publish
  the reasoned dissent of any member who requests it, unedited, alongside the
  decision. A unanimous decision must state that it was unanimous — Elysians
  read that as a warning sign (`cult.foundations` §5).
- **No closed sessions** except on active security operations and personal
  privacy, both time-limited and reviewable.
- **The reasons requirement.** Every exercise of public power must publish the
  reasons for it, in a form a non-specialist can read.

## 3. Rights

Charter rights bind every tier of government and are enforceable by any court.
They are grouped as **liberties**, **provisions**, and **procedural
guarantees** — the distinction matters because provisions are subject to
resource limits and the others are not.

**Liberties (non-derogable — cannot be suspended even in emergency):**
1. Freedom of belief and non-belief; no established religion, no religious test.
2. Freedom of expression, association, and assembly.
3. Freedom of movement and residence anywhere in the Concord; no internal
   borders, no internal citizenship (`hist.demographics` §5).
4. Bodily autonomy, including reproductive autonomy. **The Charter expressly
   forbids any birth quota, incentive, or restriction** (`hist.demographics` §2).
5. Freedom from torture, from arbitrary detention, and from capital punishment
   (abolished at founding; Phase 5).
6. Equality before the law without distinction of region, language, belief, sex,
   ancestry, or disability.
7. Privacy of person, home, communications, and data.

**Provisions (subject to resource limits, enforceable as floors):**
8. Housing adequate to health and dignity (Phase 10).
9. Healthcare (Phase 9).
10. Education, including lifelong education (Phase 8).
11. Subsistence income sufficient for participation in civic life (Phase 6).
12. Access to the commons — clean air and water, and access to protected land
    and coast.

**Procedural guarantees:**
13. **The right to be governed in your own language** (`cult.languages` §4).
14. **The right of access to record** — any person may obtain any record of any
    public body, with narrow, published, and appealable exceptions.
15. **The right to reasons** — to be told why a decision affecting you was made.
16. Fair hearing, presumption of innocence, and appeal (Phase 5).

Rights 1–7 are **non-derogable**: no emergency, referendum, or amendment short
of a full Tier 1 procedure can touch them, and Article 12 forbids even a Tier 1
amendment that would abolish the amendment procedure itself.

## 4. Entrenchment and Amendment

The Charter has three tiers of entrenchment. Rigidity is graded to consequence.

| Tier | Covers | Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| **Tier 1 — Foundational** | Rights 1–7, ecological limits, the Abolition, the amendment procedure, the withdrawal procedure | Two-thirds of the Assembly **and** two-thirds of the Council of Regions, **and** a planetary referendum, **and** majorities in at least two-thirds of Regions |
| **Tier 2 — Structural** | Institutional design, distribution of competences, independent offices | Two-thirds of both chambers **and** majorities in a majority of Regions |
| **Tier 3 — Constitutional statute** | Electoral rules, procedural detail, the registered-language list | Three-fifths of the Assembly, Council concurrence |

Every amendment at any tier passes through a **Review Panel of 300 citizens
chosen by lot**, which sits for 90 days, takes evidence in public, and publishes
a reasoned opinion. The opinion does not bind, but no amendment may be voted on
until it is published, and no amendment has ever passed against a Review Panel's
opposition.

**Thirty-one amendments** have been adopted in 412 years. The rate is
deliberately slow and Elysians consider it neither a virtue nor a defect but a
consequence of the tiers.

## 5. Withdrawal

Two Regions attempted to leave the Concord, in EY 22 and EY 31
(`hist.timeline` §10). Both were resolved by negotiation, and the second
produced the **Fourth Amendment**, which did something most constitutions do
not: it made withdrawal legal.

A Region may leave by:
1. A regional referendum carried by an absolute majority of the electorate
   (not merely of votes cast);
2. A four-year cooling period, during which the Region continues to participate
   fully and the terms of separation are negotiated and published;
3. A second referendum, carried on the same threshold, on the negotiated terms;
4. Settlement of shared obligations — grid, orbital, ecological, and pension
   commitments — with the Constitutional Court as arbiter.

Certain things cannot be withdrawn: obligations under Article 12 (a departing
Region's atmosphere is still everyone's atmosphere), the Abolition, and the
rights of residents who choose to remain Concord citizens.

**No Region has attempted withdrawal since the Fourth Amendment was adopted.**
Concord political scientists regard the procedure as the single most successful
device in the Charter, and their explanation is that a permitted exit removes the
grievance that drives most secession, while the four-year cooling period ensures
no Region can leave on a bad mood. Two Regions have opened the procedure and
both abandoned it before the second referendum.

## 6. Emergency Powers

The Emergency of EY 233 (`hist.timeline` §10) is the reason this section is the
most tightly drafted in the Charter. Emergency powers exist, are real, and are
caged.

- **Declaration.** The Executive may declare a planetary emergency, stating the
  specific powers invoked and the specific threat. A declaration that does not
  name both is void.
- **Automatic lapse at 30 days**, unless the Assembly extends it by two-thirds.
- **Absolute cap of 180 days.** Extension beyond 180 days requires a planetary
  referendum. There is no exception, including for war.
- **Automatic judicial review.** The Constitutional Court reviews every
  declaration within 7 days whether or not anyone challenges it. Nobody has to
  have standing; the review happens by operation of law.
- **Non-derogable rights stand.** Rights 1–7 are unaffected by any emergency.
- **No self-perpetuation.** Emergency powers may not be used to postpone an
  election, dissolve a chamber, alter the Charter, or extend any officeholder's
  term. An emergency declared within 90 days of a scheduled election
  automatically triggers Court supervision of that election.
- **Mandatory post-mortem.** Within 180 days of an emergency ending, the Audit
  Service publishes an unedited review of every power exercised, and the
  Executive must respond in public to each finding.

Fourteen planetary emergencies have been declared since EY 1: eleven for
disasters or epidemics, two for grid failures, and one — EY 233 — that became a
constitutional crisis in its own right.

## 7. Interpretation

The Charter contains an unusual instruction to its own interpreters
(Article 41), which Elysian jurists call **the humility clause**:

> *This Charter was written by people who could not see the future and did not
> claim to. Where its words and its purposes diverge, prefer its purposes; where
> its purposes are unclear, prefer the reading that leaves the most room to
> correct a mistake.*

In practice this makes Concord constitutional law strongly purposive and biased
toward reversibility. A reading that forecloses future correction is disfavoured
even when textually available — the clearest constitutional expression of the
civic virtue of repair (`cult.foundations` §1).

## 8. Known Weaknesses

Canon records the Charter's acknowledged failure modes, because Article 15 would
require it even if the design did not.

| Weakness | Nature | Current mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Slow amendment** | Tier 1 rigidity means a genuinely necessary change could arrive too late | Emergency powers, and the humility clause's bias to reversibility |
| **Court dependence** | Ambiguity resolves downward *because the Court says so*; a captured Court could reverse decades of decentralization | 18-year non-renewable terms, sortition-based appointment (§6 of `gov.institutions`) |
| **Floors without ceilings** | The Concord can require minimum standards but cannot prevent a wealthy Region racing far ahead, widening interregional inequality | Fiscal equalization (Phase 6), which is politically contested every year |
| **Translation dependence** | Equal authenticity of 41 texts depends on translation infrastructure (`cult.languages` §4) | Human-reviewable legal translation, Phase 13 |
| **Consensus drift** | A political culture that prizes candour and dissent can mistake performance of dissent for the substance of it | No structural mitigation; recognised and unsolved |
