## 1. Stages

| Stage | Ages | Enrolment | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early support | 0–3 EY | — | Home-based, with health visiting and paid leave (Phase 9) |
| **Commune Early Years** | 3–6 EY | 247 million | Universal, free, play-based, in the Commune |
| **Foundation School** | 6–12 EY | 495 million | General education, no selection |
| **Upper School** | 12–16 EY | 330 million | Broad curriculum to legal majority |
| Post-secondary and lifelong | 16 EY onward | 390 million | `edu.lifelong` |

**1.07 billion Elysians are in school** at any moment — 14.8% of the population.
Education is delivered by Districts (`gov.regions` §4), funded regionally, and
constrained by a Concord floor that specifies outcomes and entitlements but never
methods or content.

**There is no selection by ability before 16 EY, anywhere in the Concord.** This
is one of the few genuinely uniform features of Elysian education, and it is a
Concord floor rather than a regional choice. Schools are comprehensive, mixed,
and local. Selective admission below 16 EY is unlawful in every Region, including
for private institutions, of which there are few — private schooling exists,
serves 2.1% of pupils, and is bound by the same non-selection rule, the same
curriculum floor, and the same inspection regime.

## 2. The School Day

The school day follows biphasic sleep (`hist.demographics` §1) rather than
fighting it:

- **First waking**: roughly 4 civil hours, holding most formal instruction.
- **The Stillness**: a genuine rest period. Younger children sleep; older ones
  rest, read, or sit quietly. It is not a study period and schools may not
  schedule instruction into it.
- **Second waking**: roughly 2.5 civil hours, weighted toward practical work,
  craft, music, sport, and project work.

Five days of the eight-day week, 32 weeks of the 48-week year
(`hist.calendar` §3). Elysian children are in school for markedly fewer hours
than Integration-era schooling required, and Elysian educational research is
unanimous that the Stillness protection is the reason attainment did not fall
when hours were cut.

## 3. The Curriculum Floor

Curricula are set regionally, and vary substantially. The Concord floor requires
only that every pupil, by 16 EY, has been taught:

1. **Full literacy in their own first language**, and working literacy in
   **Concordial**. Neither may be substituted for the other, and no pupil may be
   taught only Concordial (`cult.languages` §4).
2. **Numeracy in both decimal and duodecimal.** Children learn both bases as a
   matter of course, because duodecimal survives in the calendar, in traditional
   measures, in music, and in ordinary speech (`hist.demographics` §1).
3. **Science, including ecological literacy** — enough to read the Ecological
   Commission's published account and understand what it says.
4. **Civics**, which on Elysium means the five civic virtues **and the conflicts
   between them** (`cult.foundations` §1). Elysian civics is not instruction in
   loyalty; it is the study of cases where candour wounds, restraint paralyses,
   and redundancy competes with stewardship. Pupils argue these and are assessed
   on the quality of the argument, not the conclusion.
5. **History, including the Concord's own failures.** Under the Public Record Act
   (`hist.timeline` §13), four events appear in every regional curriculum without
   exception: the **Cassian Incident**, the **Corran Scandal**, the **Emergency of
   EY 233**, and the **Serrance Storm Failure**. A curriculum omitting any of them
   fails inspection.
6. **Practical craft and repair.** Every pupil learns to fix things — textiles,
   electronics, mechanics, food, growing. This is not vocational preparation;
   it is the civic virtue of repair taught with hands (`cult.foundations` §1).

Everything else is regional. Some Regions teach four languages, some teach deep
mathematics early, the Isle Regions teach navigation and open-water competence to
every child, and Veydra teaches field science from 8 EY. The Concord regards this
variation as a research asset and publishes comparative outcomes without ranking
Regions against a single scale.

## 4. Assessment

**There is no high-stakes terminal examination anywhere in the Concord.** No
single test determines a pupil's future, and the Concord floor prohibits any
Region from creating one.

Assessment is continuous and portfolio-based: a body of work assembled over
years, assessed by the pupil's own teachers and **externally moderated** by
assessors from other Districts who never meet the pupil. Moderation samples are
published in anonymised form so the standard itself is inspectable.

The reasoning is characteristically Elysian and appears in the founding statute:
a single examination is a **single point of failure** in a young person's life,
and a system that concentrates a decision at one moment will eventually put a
catastrophe there. The same sentence appears in the design rationale for the
Executive Board (`gov.institutions` §3).

The honest cost is in §7: portfolio assessment is harder to compare across
Regions, slower, and more dependent on the integrity of moderation than an
examination would be.

## 5. Teachers

| Indicator | Value |
|---|---|
| Educators, all stages | 94 million |
| Pupil–teacher ratio, Foundation | 14:1 |
| Formation | 5 Elysian years, salaried from year 2 |
| Median pay relative to median earnings | 1.3× |
| Applicants per training place | 3.4 |

Teaching is a high-status profession on Elysium, paid above the median, and
competitive to enter. Formation is long — five years, of which two are
supervised practice — and is treated as a professional qualification comparable
to medicine or law.

**The eighth term.** Every teacher spends one term in eight outside the
classroom: in research, in industry, in another school, in a Mediation House, or
in public administration, on full pay with a substitute funded. It is compulsory,
not a privilege, and its stated purpose is to prevent a profession that teaches
about the world from losing contact with it. Teachers describe it variously as
the best thing about the job and an administrative nightmare.

Teachers may not be dismissed for the attainment of their pupils, and no Region
publishes teacher-level outcome data. School-level data is published; the Concord
floor forbids disaggregating it to individuals, on the reasoning that a measure
attached to a person becomes a target for that person.

## 6. Inspection

Schools are inspected by Regional inspectorates that must be independent of the
District operating the school — the audit principle (`gov.constitution` §2.2)
applied to education. Inspection reports publish in full, including dissent
within the inspection team.

An inspectorate may require a school to change and may replace its leadership. It
may not close a school without the consent of the Commune it serves, which is the
one place Elysian education law gives a local body an absolute veto over a
regional authority. Communes have used it 41 times in the last decade, and the
schools concerned have mostly improved.

## 7. Known Weaknesses

| Weakness | Nature |
|---|---|
| **Portfolio comparability** | Assessment without examinations is genuinely harder to compare across 34 Regions; employers and universities rely on institutional reputation to fill the gap, which reintroduces exactly the signalling the system was built to avoid |
| **Moderation integrity** | The whole system rests on external moderators; the Audit Service has found moderation drift in nine Regions and describes the risk as structural rather than incidental |
| **Regional divergence** | Floors are floors. Attainment in the strongest and weakest Regions differs by roughly the equivalent of two years of schooling, and equalization narrows the funding gap without closing the outcome gap |
| **Teacher supply in small Regions** | Northreach, Austral Shore, and Highmarch run persistent vacancies; shared regional services are the mitigation and are criticised as centralization |
| **The eighth term is unevenly honoured** | Compliance runs at 78%; small schools cannot easily release staff and quietly do not |
| **Private schooling** | Only 2.1% of pupils and heavily constrained, but concentrated among high-wealth households, and its graduates are over-represented in some professions by a margin nobody has explained convincingly |
